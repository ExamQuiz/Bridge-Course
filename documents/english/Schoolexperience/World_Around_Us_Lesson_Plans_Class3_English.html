How to use these plans

  • These 10 plans are written for Class 3. Change the examples, contexts and difficulty level for Classes 1 to 5.
  • Each lesson starts from the children’s own experience and surroundings, moves ahead through activity and investigation, and arrives at the children’s own conclusions. Every plan links to language, mathematics, art or sport, because this subject joins different subjects together.
  • The materials are low-cost or no-cost: seeds, pebbles, leaves, bottle caps, clay, paper and chalk. Use whatever is available in your school and community.
  • Each plan has space for the curricular goal and competency number. Fill it in from your textbook or curriculum.
  • The assessment section gives children’s common alternate conceptions. Instead of calling them “wrong” directly, give children a chance to test them, because the course has a separate chapter on understanding children’s ideas and alternate conceptions.
  • Supervise water, seed and outdoor activities. Do not let anyone disturb or harm any living thing, and do not let children look directly at the sun.
  • Children may explain their thinking in the language in which they are comfortable. Teach new words gradually and use them repeatedly.
  • Your supervisor will assess five lessons in each subject. Keep all 10 plans ready in the file and fill in the reflection after every lesson.

Lesson plan 1

What Is Around Us? (Look, Listen, Touch)

ClassIII (Class 3)SubjectThe World Around Us
AreaObservation and curiosityDuration40 minutes
TopicObserving by looking, listening, touching and smelling, and asking questions
Date Teacher 
Goal / competency no.(fill in from your textbook or curriculum)

Curricular goal

Children collect information about things around them by looking, listening, touching and smelling carefully, and ask questions out of curiosity.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, children:

  • Tell the difference between the information that comes from the eyes, ears, nose and skin.
  • Describe a thing using words for colour, shape, sound, texture and smell, such as soft, rough, sharp.
  • Ask at least two questions based on what they observed, such as “Why is it like this?”

Teaching-learning materials

  • A cloth bag with 5-6 everyday things inside (a leaf, chalk, a pebble, cotton wool, a mint leaf or a piece of lemon peel)
  • A handkerchief to cover the eyes
  • An observation sheet for children (sample given below)
  • Chart paper, slips of paper and colours

Prior knowledge

Children know the names of body parts and everyday objects.

Teaching process

StageTeaching activityTime
OpeningAsk children to close their eyes and sit quietly for one minute, listening to the sounds. Then ask, “Which sounds did you hear?” Make a list on the board and ask, “How did we know even with our eyes closed?”5 minutes
Mystery bagIn pairs, one child covers their eyes with a handkerchief, touches a thing in the bag and says whether it is hard or soft, smooth or rough. The partner writes down their words. Then smell one item and describe its smell. Swap roles.10 minutes
Look-around walkPairs walk around the classroom or ground for five minutes and fill in the observation sheet: one soft thing, one rough thing, one sound, one smell. Ask children to walk slowly and not to break or damage anything.10 minutes
SharingPairs share what they found. The teacher makes a table on the board by sense. Each child writes one question on a slip and puts it on the “Question Wall”. Read out a few questions and ask, “How can we find this out?”8 minutes
ExpressionChildren draw their favourite thing and write two sentences about how it looks, feels or smells. Then play the “Who am I?” riddle game: a child gives clues and the class guesses.7 minutes

Observation sheet (for children)

I saw: ____________________

I heard: ____________________

I touched (soft or rough): ____________________

I smelt: ____________________

My question: ____________________

Assessment

  • Observe: Does the child use more than one sense? Does the child choose the right words to describe? Does the child ask questions of their own?
  • Note common alternate conceptions, such as believing that information comes only from seeing, or treating a liking like “it is beautiful” as an observation. Help children see the difference between “it is soft” (a fact) and “I like it” (an opinion).

Inclusion and extra support

  • Let children who have difficulty seeing lead the touching, listening and smelling activities. For children who have difficulty hearing, emphasise looking and touching.
  • If a child does not like a smell, do not force them to smell it.
  • Let children speak in the language in which they are comfortable.

Extension / homework

At home, sit quietly for two minutes, listen for three sounds and write them down or tell someone. Also find one thing you had never paid attention to before.

Reflection after the lesson (teacher fills in)

What went well?

Which children found it difficult?

What will I change next time?

Where Does Water Come From?

ClassIII (Class 3)SubjectThe World Around Us
AreaWaterDuration40 minutes
TopicSources of water, its journey to our homes and saving water
Date Teacher 
Goal / competency no.(fill in from your textbook or curriculum)

Curricular goal

Children understand sources and uses of water by connecting them to their own experience and develop a sense of responsibility towards water.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, children:

  • Name at least four sources of water, such as rain, river, pond, well and hand pump.
  • Arrange picture cards in order to show how water travels from its source to the tap at home.
  • Estimate the quantity of water by filling a bucket with a mug, and state two ways to save water.

Teaching-learning materials

  • Picture cards of water sources (rain, river, pond, well, hand pump, tank, tap)
  • Bucket, mug and water
  • Chart paper, colours and pencils
  • Chalk and blackboard

Prior knowledge

Children use water every day and know some water sources around them.

Teaching process

StageTeaching activityTime
OpeningA child pretends to open a tap and fill water. Ask, “Where did this water come from?” Write all of the children’s ideas on the board without saying right or wrong. We will look at them again at the end of the lesson.5 minutes
Identify the sourcesGive groups the picture cards. They choose the sources visible in their village or neighbourhood and tell what people take water from them for: drinking, cooking, bathing, farming or animals.8 minutes
Water’s journeyGroups arrange the sequence cards given below in the right order. Then children stand in a line and act out “the journey of a drop of water”: rain, river or well, tank, pipe and tap.10 minutes
How much water?Children fill a bucket using a mug and count how many mugs it takes. Ask, “How many buckets might our home use in a day?” Then discuss which ways of saving water we can adopt: closing the tap while brushing teeth, bathing with a bucket, pouring leftover water on plants.10 minutes
Expression and revisitChildren make a “Save Water” poster or a two-line slogan. Then look again at the ideas written on the board at the start and ask, “What do we think differently now?”7 minutes

The journey of water: sequence cards (sample)

1. Rain water collects in rivers, ponds and under the ground.

2. Water is drawn from the river, a well or from under the ground (using a motor or a hand pump).

3. The water is cleaned and filled into a tank.

4. From the tank, water goes to homes through pipes.

5. When we open the tap, we get water.

Assessment

  • Observe: Can the child name at least four sources? Can the child arrange the sequence cards correctly? Does the child give their own examples of saving water?
  • Note common alternate conceptions, such as “water is made inside the tap” or “water that looks clear is always safe to drink.” Keep the children’s first ideas safe and let them compare them at the end of the lesson.

Inclusion and extra support

  • Listen respectfully to children who fetch water from far every day and treat them as a source of information for the class. Do not comment on any child’s home situation.
  • Give children who find it hard to lift a heavy bucket the role of counting mugs or writing.
  • Let children speak in the language in which they are comfortable.

Extension / homework

At home, ask elders where the water in your house comes from. Make a list of five uses of water in one day and think of a way to save water in one of them.

Reflection after the lesson (teacher fills in)

What went well?

Which children found it difficult?

What will I change next time?

From Seed to Plant

ClassIII (Class 3)SubjectThe World Around Us
AreaPlantsDuration40 minutes
TopicHow a seed sprouts and what a plant needs to grow
Date Teacher 
Goal / competency no.(fill in from your textbook or curriculum)

Curricular goal

Through an experiment and observation over several days, children understand how a seed grows and what a plant needs.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, children:

  • Identify the root, stem and leaf in a sprouted seed and draw them.
  • Guess what a plant needs to grow and begin an experiment with three containers.
  • Measure the length of the root with a thread or fingers and record it in the observation table.

Teaching-learning materials

  • Dry seeds and seeds soaked overnight (gram or moong)
  • Sprouted seeds that the teacher has kept in a damp cloth one, two and three days earlier
  • Three containers or bowls, soil, water and thread
  • Magnifying glass (if available), chart paper and colours

Prior knowledge

Children have seen seeds and plants and know the names of a few plants.

Teaching process

StageTeaching activityTime
OpeningShow a dry seed and ask, “Is there life in this? What does it need to become a plant?” Write the children’s guesses (soil, water, sunlight, air, manure) on a chart.5 minutes
ObservationGroups look at dry seeds, soaked seeds and seeds sprouted for one, two and three days. They measure the root length with a thread and gently open a soaked seed to look inside. They draw each stage.10 minutes
Setting up the experimentEach group prepares three containers: (a) soil, water and sunlight; (b) soil and sunlight, but no water; (c) soil and water, but in a dark place (a cupboard or under a cardboard box). Children first write their guess about what will happen in each container.10 minutes
DiscussionChildren share their guesses. Ask, “Do plants grow by eating soil? How can we check this?” Do not give an answer yet; say we will see the results after a few days.8 minutes
Seed diaryChildren draw “from seed to plant” in four boxes and fill in today’s date and their guesses in the observation table. Decide which group will make which observation over the next seven days.7 minutes

Observation table (for children)

ContainerMy guessDay 3Day 5Day 7
A: soil, water, sunlight    
B: no water    
C: in the dark    

Assessment

  • Observe: Does the child identify the root, stem and leaf? Does the child write a guess first and compare it with the results later? Does the child record changes by measuring or drawing?
  • Note common alternate conceptions, such as “plants grow by eating soil.” In Class 3, say only that a plant needs water, sunlight and air to grow, and let children draw the results from their own experiment, such as the plant in the dark container looking pale and weak.

Inclusion and extra support

  • Give children who find small seeds hard to hold rajma or large gram seeds.
  • Give everyone turns at measuring, drawing, writing and watering.
  • Let children describe their observations in the language in which they are comfortable.

Extension / homework

At home, put a few fenugreek or mustard seeds in a damp cloth and draw them every day. After five days, tell what changed.

What went well?

Which children found it difficult?

What will I change next time?

Lesson plan 4

Animals and Their Homes

ClassIII (Class 3)SubjectThe World Around Us
AreaLiving thingsDuration40 minutes
TopicGrouping animals on a basis of their own, and the homes animals live in
Date Teacher 
Goal / competency no.(fill in from your textbook or curriculum)

Curricular goal

Children observe animals around them, notice similarities and differences, and make groups on a basis of their own.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, children:

  • Make groups of animals on a basis of their own (where they live, how they move or what they eat) and state the basis.
  • Name the homes of at least five animals, such as nest, burrow, hive and den.
  • State that insects, birds and fish are also animals.

Teaching-learning materials

  • 10-12 animal picture cards (cow, dog, sparrow, butterfly, ant, fish, frog, snake, honeybee, squirrel)
  • Picture cards of homes (nest, burrow, hive, den, pond, shed)
  • Chart paper and chalk
  • Notebook and colours for drawing

Prior knowledge

Children know the names and sounds of some animals around them.

Teaching process

StageTeaching activityTime
OpeningPlay “Be an animal”: a child acts out the movement of an animal (hopping, crawling, flying) and the others guess. Ask, “Do all of them move in the same way?”5 minutes
Sort the cardsGive groups the picture cards and ask them to sort them into two or three groups. They choose the basis themselves. Each group shows its groups and the other groups guess what the basis was. Then sort the same cards on a different basis.10 minutes
Animal homesChildren match the animal cards with the home cards. Ask, “Why is this home right for this animal?” Discuss how some animals build their own homes (birds, honeybees, ants) and for some, humans build homes (cow, dog).10 minutes
Look-around walkChildren watch living things from a distance in the school premises for five minutes (ant, butterfly, squirrel, crow). They do not disturb or touch them. On returning, they tell their partner what they saw, where it was and what it was doing.8 minutes
Riddle and drawingEach child writes three clues about their favourite animal to make a riddle and the class guesses. Then the child draws that animal and its home.7 minutes

Bases for grouping (hints for the teacher)

Where they live: on land, in water, on trees or in the air.

How they move: walking, flying, crawling, swimming or hopping.

What they eat: grass and leaves, other animals or both.

How many legs: two, four, six or none.

Children may also choose another basis. Accept every sensible basis.

Assessment

  • Observe: Can the child state their basis clearly? Can the child sort the same cards on another basis too? Does the child match animals and homes correctly?
  • Note common alternate conceptions, such as “an animal means only a four-legged creature” or “insects and birds are not animals.” Take the discussion forward from the groups the children made themselves.

Inclusion and extra support

  • Give children who are shy about acting the role of sorting cards or writing the riddle.
  • Some children are afraid of dogs, snakes or insects. Do not make fun of their fear and let them watch from a distance.
  • Let children also say the names of animals in their home language.

Extension / homework

Watch one animal or insect near your home from a distance for five minutes. Draw what it eats, how it moves and where it lives, and bring the drawing.

Reflection after the lesson (teacher fills in)

What went well?

Which children found it difficult?

What will I change next time?

Lesson plan 5

Where Does Our Food Come From?

ClassIII (Class 3)SubjectThe World Around Us
AreaFoodDuration40 minutes
TopicSources of food, the journey from farm to plate and respect for food
Date Teacher 
Goal / competency no.(fill in from your textbook or curriculum)

Curricular goal

Children connect the food on their plate to its source and learn to respect food and the people who grow and make it.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, children:

  • Put food items into the groups “from plants”, “from animals” and “from both”.
  • Name the plant parts we eat (root, leaf, fruit, seed), such as carrot, spinach, mango and gram.
  • Tell the steps of wheat chapati from the farm to the plate in order.

Teaching-learning materials

  • Real or picture food items (carrot, spinach, mango or banana, gram, milk, flour)
  • A plate drawn on chart paper, and colours
  • Sequence cards from farm to plate (sample given below)
  • Glue, scissors and old magazines (if available)

Prior knowledge

Children eat food every day and can name some foods they like.

Teaching process

StageTeaching activityTime
OpeningAsk, “What did you eat this morning?” Write the answers on the board. Then ask, “Where might each of these things have come from?” Listen to the children’s ideas.5 minutes
My plateEach child draws or pastes pictures of their food on a plate on chart paper. Then in groups they sort the items into “from plants”, “from animals” and “from both”. Every family’s food may be different; treat this as normal.10 minutes
Which part of the plantLooking at real items or pictures, children say which part of the plant we eat in a carrot (root), spinach (leaf), mango (fruit) and gram (seed). Arrange items with different plant parts in “our vegetable basket”.8 minutes
From farm to plateFour or five groups each choose one step of the sequence cards and act it out without speaking: farmer, harvesters, flour-mill worker, person working in the kitchen. The class stands the steps in the right order. Let both boys and girls play all the roles.10 minutes
Thanks and closingDiscuss taking only as much food on the plate as we can eat. Each child says one sentence, such as “I thank all the people whose hard work gave me food.”7 minutes

From farm to plate: sequence cards (wheat chapati)

1. The farmer sows wheat seeds in the field.

2. The crop grows and ripens.

3. The wheat is harvested and the grains are separated.

4. The grains are ground in a mill to make flour.

5. At home, the flour is kneaded and chapatis are made.

6. The chapati is served on the plate.

Assessment

  • Observe: Does the child put items in the right group? Does the child identify plant parts? Does the child tell the sequence correctly?
  • Note common alternate conceptions, such as “milk, flour or vegetables are made in shops” or “cooking is only women’s work.” Discuss these using the children’s own experience and show that everyone has a role in kitchen and farm work.

Inclusion and extra support

  • Respect differences in families’ food (vegetarian, non-vegetarian, fasting or other traditions). Do not call any food good or bad.
  • Some children do not get a full meal every day. Be sensitive when asking questions on this topic and do not force anyone to talk about their plate.
  • Let children speak in the language in which they are comfortable.

Extension / homework

At home, watch or help while a dish is being made. Write its three ingredients and tell whether each came from a plant or an animal.

Reflection after the lesson (teacher fills in)

What went well?

Which children found it difficult?

What will I change next time?

Lesson plan 6

Will It Float or Sink?

ClassIII (Class 3)SubjectThe World Around Us
AreaObjects and materialsDuration40 minutes
TopicFloating and sinking of objects: guess, experiment and conclusion
Date Teacher 
Goal / competency no.(fill in from your textbook or curriculum)

Curricular goal

Children learn to think scientifically by guessing, testing and drawing conclusions from the results.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, children:

  • Guess first whether eight objects will float or sink, then test and record the results.
  • Compare their guesses with the results and state that heavy objects do not always sink.
  • Make a clay ball and a clay boat and say that changing the shape can change the result.

Teaching-learning materials

  • A bucket or large plate full of water (one for each group)
  • Pebble, coin, dry leaf, piece of wood, plastic bottle cap, eraser, chalk, sponge
  • Kneaded clay and a cloth for wiping
  • Observation table (sample given below)

Prior knowledge

Children have played by putting things into water.

Teaching process

StageTeaching activityTime
OpeningShow a pebble and a leaf and ask, “What will happen if we put these in water?” Listen to a few children’s guesses and ask, “How can we find out the truth?” Lead the talk towards “let’s try and see.”5 minutes
GuessGive groups eight objects. In the table, against each object they write a guess (will float or will sink) and a reason: “Why did I think so?”7 minutes
TestOne by one, gently put each object in water and write the result in the table. Put a tick for a correct guess and a question mark where the result was different. Stop and talk about surprising results.10 minutes
ChallengeMake a clay ball and put it in water. Then make a boat from the same clay and try it. Place pebbles in the boat one at a time and count how many pebbles it floats with. Ask, “Why was the result different for the same clay?”10 minutes
ConclusionEach group states one conclusion in their own words. Make a class table and ask, “Which guess turned out most wrong? What new thing did we learn?” Write it in the children’s words.8 minutes

Observation table (sample)

ObjectMy guessResultWhat did I think?
Pebble

Assessment

  • Observe: Does the child record the guess and the result separately? Does the child draw the conclusion from the results? Can the child connect the boat result to its shape?
  • Note common alternate conceptions, such as “heavy things always sink and light things always float.” Instead of correcting a wrong guess directly, say, “Let’s test and see.”

Inclusion and extra support

  • Give children who are uncomfortable around water the role of writing the table or asking for guesses, and give everyone a chance to touch the objects.
  • Do not scold children if water spills; let everyone take turns wiping it up.
  • Let children speak in the language in which they are comfortable.

Extension / homework

Under adult supervision, try five things in a bucket or tub at home. Write your guess first and then the result. Count and tell how many guesses were right.

Lesson plan 7

Helpers in Our Neighbourhood

ClassIII (Class 3)SubjectThe World Around Us
AreaCommunity and peopleDuration40 minutes
TopicPeople in the community and their work, an interview, and respect for every kind of work
Date Teacher 
Goal / competency no.(fill in from your textbook or curriculum)

Curricular goal

Children understand the work of the people around them, see the community as a resource for learning, and learn to respect every kind of work.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, children:

  • Tell about at least five helpers and their work, such as the postman, sweeper, gardener, shopkeeper and nurse.
  • Say what would happen if these jobs were not done, and use this to explain why every job is needed.
  • Make three questions, talk to one helper, and write or draw their answers.

Teaching-learning materials

  • Picture cards of helpers
  • Invite a school staff member (gardener, cook, sweeper) or a parent as a guest in advance
  • Slips with interview questions (sample given below)
  • Chart paper, colours and paper for thank-you cards

Prior knowledge

Children know some people in their neighbourhood and their work.

Teaching process

StageTeaching activityTime
OpeningAsk a riddle: “I clean the street early in the morning and pick up the rubbish. Who am I?” Children guess. Then put up the cards of the other helpers on the board.5 minutes
Acting out the workGroups act out one helper without speaking and the others guess. Then ask, “What are their tools? Where do they work?”8 minutes
What if they were not here?Ask, “If for one week the sweeper, shopkeeper or doctor did not come to our neighbourhood, what would happen?” Listen to the children’s ideas and reach the conclusion that every job is needed and no job is small or big.7 minutes
InterviewThe class makes three questions together. When the guest arrives, children take turns to ask the questions. The other children listen to the answers and write or draw in their notebooks. Ask the guest what they could show the children about their work.13 minutes
Thanks and closingChildren make thank-you cards for the guest and complete the sentence “You help us because…” in it.7 minutes

Sample interview questions

A. What work do you do?

B. When and how do you start your day?

C. What do you like about your work?

D. What difficulties do you face while working?

E. How can we children help you?

Assessment

  • Observe: Does the child describe the work of five helpers correctly? Does the child ask questions respectfully, using “why” or “how”? Does the child record the answers in their own words?
  • Note common alternate conceptions, such as “some jobs are small or less important” or “some jobs are done only by men or only by women.” Show children examples that go against these ideas.

Inclusion and extra support

  • When choosing guests, include people from different jobs, backgrounds and genders. Do not let any child’s parents’ work be made fun of.
  • Shy children can prepare the questions in a group first and then ask them.
  • Children who find writing difficult can draw to show the answers.

Extension / homework

At home or in the neighbourhood, watch one person’s work carefully and write three sentences or draw what they do and what we get from them.

Reflection after the lesson (teacher fills in)

What went well?

Which children found it difficult?

What will I change next time?

Lesson plan 8

A Map of My Classroom

ClassIII (Class 3)SubjectThe World Around Us
AreaPlace and directionDuration40 minutes
TopicMaking a map by looking from above, and describing positions
Date Teacher 
Goal / competency no.(fill in from your textbook or curriculum)

Curricular goal

Children learn to show their surroundings on a map by looking from above and to use words that tell position.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, children:

  • Tell the position of objects using words such as left, right, in front, behind, near and between.
  • Think how four or five objects in the classroom would look from above and show them on a map using symbols.
  • Measure a distance in steps or handspans and say it, such as eight steps from the door to the blackboard.

Teaching-learning materials

  • Large chart paper, notebooks, pencils and colours
  • Classroom objects (desk, bench, blackboard, door, window)
  • A glass, a book and a box
  • A small object to hide (the “treasure”)

Prior knowledge

Children know left and right and can name things in the classroom.

Teaching process

StageTeaching activityTime
OpeningPlay “Treasure hunt”. The teacher gives directions to reach an object hidden in the classroom, such as “three steps forward, two steps left.” Later, children take turns to give directions.5 minutes
Looking from aboveLook at the glass, book and box first from the front and then from above. Ask, “If a bird looked from above, how would these look?” Children draw both views in their notebooks (from above, the glass looks round and the book looks rectangular).8 minutes
Steps and handspansPairs count steps from the door to the blackboard and measure the length of a desk in handspans. Write the results on the board and see why different children’s steps are different.8 minutes
Make the mapGroups make a map of the classroom on chart paper by looking from above: door, blackboard, window, teacher’s table and children’s benches. They make their own symbols and write what they mean in a corner of the map.12 minutes
PresentationOne group shows its map. Children from another group look at the map and tell the position of an object in words, such as “The teacher’s table is to the left of the door.” The class checks whether it matches the real classroom.7 minutes

Suggestions for making symbols

Children can decide their own symbols, such as a rectangle for a bench, a circle for a water pot and a thick line for the blackboard.

Always make a “Key” box in every map, with the meaning of each symbol written in it.

On a map, the correct position of things matters more than their real size.

Assessment

  • Observe: Does the child draw as seen from above, or draw a front view? Does the child use position words correctly? Does the child write the meaning of the symbols?
  • Note common alternate conceptions, such as “a map is a front-view picture of things” or “every object on a map must be drawn at its real size.” Also watch for mix-ups between left and right, especially when describing from the point of view of a partner standing opposite.

Inclusion and extra support

  • For children who have difficulty with left and right, tie a coloured thread or put a mark on their left hand.
  • For children with visual impairment, have a raised map made with thread or dry leaves and give them the role of counting steps and telling positions.
  • Let children speak in the language in which they are comfortable.

Extension / homework

Draw a map of one room at home by looking from above. Then describe the way from home to school with three landmarks (such as a shop, a tree, a water tank).

Reflection after the lesson (teacher fills in)

What went well?

Which children found it difficult?

What will I change next time?

Lesson plan 9

The Weather Diary

ClassIII (Class 3)SubjectThe World Around Us
AreaWeather and our lifeDuration40 minutes
TopicObserving and recording the weather, and linking the textbook to local experience
Date Teacher 
Goal / competency no.(fill in from your textbook or curriculum)

Curricular goal

Children understand the weather through regular observation and recording, and connect textbook content to their local experience.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, children:

  • Observe the sky, wind and heat or cold and record the weather with picture symbols.
  • Read a five-day weather table and state simple conclusions, such as “It was sunny for three days.”
  • Say that our clothes, food and games change with the weather.

Teaching-learning materials

  • A textbook lesson or picture about weather or seasons (choose from your own textbook)
  • A weather table drawn on chart paper (sample given below)
  • Symbol cards for sun, clouds, rain and wind
  • A thin strip of paper (to see the wind), colours and glue

Prior knowledge

Children can talk about their experiences of summer, winter and rain.

Teaching process

StageTeaching activityTime
OpeningTake children outside or near a window. Ask, “What is the weather like today?” They look at the sky (not directly at the sun), recognise the wind with a paper strip, and tell the difference by placing a hand in sun and in shade.5 minutes
Linking to the textbookLook at the weather picture or lesson in the textbook. Ask, “Which weather is shown in this picture? How did you know? What is different here?” Explain that the pictures and questions in the book are for us to look around us. Link children’s local examples with what the book says.10 minutes
Weather tableMake the class weather table and fill in the weather of the last five days with the symbol cards. Choose a “weather reporter” for each day, who will fill in the table every day.8 minutes
Read the table and thinkAsk questions from the table: How many days were sunny? How many days were cloudy? Which weather lasted the most days? Then do a matching activity with cards: which weather, which clothes, which food and which game. Ask, “What might the weather be like tomorrow? Why?”10 minutes
ClosingChildren together make a short song or poem about the weather, such as “The sun is out, let’s go and play…”, and tell which weather they like best and why.7 minutes

Assessment

  • Observe: Does the child record the weather correctly with symbols? Does the child count from the table and state conclusions? Can the child connect the textbook picture to their own experience?
  • Note common alternate conceptions, such as “clouds are made of smoke or cotton” (they are made of tiny water droplets) or “in summer the sun comes closer to us.” Do not debate these in Class 3; put the children’s ideas on the “Question Wall” and keep them for later investigation.

Inclusion and extra support

  • Not everyone has clothes for every kind of weather. Do not ask who has what; have a general talk about what people wear.
  • Stop children from looking directly at the sun and do not keep them in the sun for long.
  • Give every child a turn as weather reporter and let them speak in their own language.

Extension / homework

For one week at home, look at the sky every morning and draw it, or pick the weather symbol for the class table. At the end, tell which weather was the most frequent this week.

Reflection after the lesson (teacher fills in)

What went well?

Which children found it difficult?

Lesson plan 10

Assessment and Review (Three Stations)

ClassIII (Class 3)SubjectThe World Around Us
AreaAssessmentDuration40 minutes
TopicA combined assessment of observation, grouping, guess-and-test and communication, with self-correction
Date Teacher 
Goal / competency no.(fill in from your textbook or curriculum)

Curricular goal

To understand each child’s learning and process skills through play and to plan further learning.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, children:

  • Describe something by looking, touching and smelling, and ask two questions.
  • Sort picture cards into groups on a basis of their own and solve the questions on the worksheet.
  • Make a guess about a simple problem and say how it could be tested.

Teaching-learning materials

  • A leaf, a fruit and a pebble for observation
  • Picture cards of animals, plants and food, and problem cards
  • Worksheet and observation checklist
  • Stickers of stars or smiles

Prior knowledge

Practice done in the previous nine lessons.

Teaching process

StageTeaching activityTime
InstructionsExplain the three stations and divide the children into three groups. Say that this is not a test, but a way of seeing how children are learning. Each group will visit every station in turn.4 minutes
Station 1: Look and tellThe teacher gives a child a leaf, a fruit or a pebble. The child looks, touches and smells it, tells three things and asks two questions. The teacher fills in the observation checklist.8 minutes
Station 2: WorksheetChildren complete the worksheet (sample given below).8 minutes
Station 3: Think and tellPairs sort the picture cards into groups on a basis of their own and state the basis. Then they read the problem card: “A plant was not given water for many days and it wilted. Why did this happen? How can we check?” The teacher listens to their thinking.8 minutes
Self-correctionPartners check each other’s worksheet against the model answers. Children correct their own mistakes and give themselves a star or a smile.8 minutes
ClosingThe teacher gives the class general feedback and tells what we will practise next.4 minutes

Sample worksheet questions

A. Write the names of two sources of water: ___ and ___.

B. Write two things needed for a seed to become a plant: ___ and ___.

C. Match the pairs: bird, ant, fish — water, hole, nest.

D. Sort these into things that float and things that sink: piece of wood, pebble, dry leaf, coin.

E. Write one thing from your plate and say whether it came from a plant or an animal.

F. Write the name of one helper in your neighbourhood and one of their jobs.

G. What is near the door in your classroom? Write one sentence using position words.

H. Show today’s weather with a drawing and tell what clothes would be right to wear today.

Assessment

  • Fill in all four areas for each child in the table given below.
  • Pick the areas in which most children are at the “Still learning” level and plan extra practice for them.
  • Note the alternate conceptions that remain and choose new activities in later classes to test them.

Inclusion and extra support

  • Give children who find writing difficult the option to answer orally or with materials.
  • Give all children enough time, and look at each child’s own progress instead of comparing them with others.

Extension / homework

At home, tell someone the most interesting thing you learned by seeing for yourself about the world around you, and what more you want to know.
